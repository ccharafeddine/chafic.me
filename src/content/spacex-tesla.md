# SpaceX / Tesla: what would a fair exchange ratio be, and who has to say yes?

*Independent case study by Chafic Charafeddine (MS Finance, University of St. Thomas, 2026; CFA Level I candidate). It uses public information only. It was not prepared for, reviewed by or affiliated with SpaceX, Tesla, their advisors, or any employer or client, and it is not investment advice. **No transaction has been agreed or proposed in a filing.** Both companies have disclosed only that special committees of independent directors are exploring a closer relationship, "including without limitation a business combination". Every exchange ratio, premium and ownership split below is a hypothetical I built from public figures to frame the negotiation, and is labelled as such. Market data are closing prices on 22-Oct-2026 (SPCX $144.75, TSLA $374.58). Share counts come from each company's Q3-2026 10-Q cover page (15-Oct-2026). Financials come from the Q3-2026 earnings releases, the 10-Qs and SpaceX's IPO prospectus (424(b)(4), Jun-2026). Musk's holdings come from the SpaceX prospectus and Tesla's 2026 proxy (DEF 14A, 19-Oct-2026).*

## Bottom line

**Status: exploratory talks only. My view is that a deal is plausible but not close, and that the premium, not the logic, will decide it.**

- **Confirmed:** both boards have formed special committees (Tesla 10-Q and SpaceX 10-Q, 22-Oct-2026). Musk has recused himself from them and called a combination "a good idea" (SpaceX Q3 call, 20-Oct). SpaceX says any deal also needs the approval of its "controlling shareholder", which is Musk. **Not public:** terms, structure, advisors, committee members and timetable.
- **At 22-Oct closing prices the two companies are worth $3.45T together:** SpaceX $1.97T and Tesla $1.48T. A zero-premium swap is **2.588 SPCX per TSLA** and would give Tesla holders 43.0% of the combined company.
- **A premium is a straight value transfer unless synergies exist.** The 20-35% that RBC and JPMorgan say Tesla holders will want works out to **$296B-$518B** of value that has to come back as capitalised synergies before SpaceX holders are made whole. At 30%, that is about $22B a year after tax on a 20x multiple (my assumption), which is more than Tesla's entire TTM operating expense base of $16.5B.
- **Contribution analysis justifies anywhere from no premium to about 33%**, depending on which earnings you believe. Tesla supplies 76% of TTM revenue and 50% of TTM adjusted EBITDA, but only 31% of Q3 annualised adjusted EBITDA.
- **Musk keeps control in any SpaceX-led structure.** If SpaceX keeps its 10-vote Class B shares, I estimate his voting power at 74-76% of the combined company, against ~20% at Tesla as of October 2026. **His economic interest runs the other way.** He owns ~45% of SpaceX and ~18% of Tesla (~18% is his 699.6M shares held over all 3,953M Tesla shares outstanding; the 19.8% beneficial-basis figure in §6 excludes the 423.7M unearned award shares from the share count), so every point of premium costs him money: about **-$61B at a 30% premium** before synergies.
- **The binding constraint is Tesla's vote:** a majority of *all outstanding* shares, by Musk's own description. That is a governance problem, not an antitrust one.

## 2. Why do it

The stated logic is shared AI and hardware. Musk named software, chip design, robotics and connectivity, and pointed to Terafab, the joint chip plant planned for Grimes County, Texas (~$16.8B first phase, per company statements). The companies are already connected in four ways:

- **Commercial:** Tesla recognised $626M of Megapack revenue from SpaceX in 9M-2026. It held $729M of receivables and $648M of deferred revenue from SpaceX at 30-Sep.
- **Capital:** Tesla's SpaceX stake was carried at $2,865M at 30-Sep, at fair value. It is subject to IPO sale restrictions until December 2026.
- **Joint projects:** Terafab, a Roadster built with SpaceX hardware, and Starlink at Superchargers.
- **People:** Musk is CEO of both. Ira Ehrenpreis sits on both boards; Tesla's proxy says he joined SpaceX's board in Feb-2026.

**My read:** the strategic overlap is real, but most of it is **already available by contract**. The test for a merger is the *incremental* value over arm's-length agreements. That means a single cost of capital for SpaceX's capex bill, less related-party friction, and one CEO's time, set against the loss of Tesla's independent governance. Musk's own phrase for the current arrangement was "an odd kabuki dance". That argues for simplification, but it also shows how much the merger itself is about control.

Neither stock trades on near-term earnings. SpaceX's case is momentum: Q3 revenue grew 161% year on year to $14.6B, SI (AI) segment revenue was $7.6B, and it reported its first quarterly net income as a public company ($4.4B, helped by $1.0B of other income). Its weakness is cash: $48.1B of capex in nine months. Tesla's case is optionality on autonomy and robotics on top of a flat core: Q3 revenue was $28.0B (-0.2% y/y) and GAAP net income $0.8B. **For the exchange ratio that matters because there is no anchor DCF either side would accept.** The negotiation will fall back on market prices and contribution.

## 4. Exchange-ratio math (hypothetical)

Market ratio = TSLA / SPCX = $374.58 / $144.75 = **2.588x** (unaffected, at the 20-Oct close before the call: $373.71 / $147.79 = 2.529x). New SPCX shares = 3,952.8M × ratio. Tesla holders' stake = new shares / (13,581M + new shares). "Gain to Tesla" assumes the combined company is worth the 22-Oct $3,446B, with no synergies.

| Premium | Ratio (SPCX per TSLA) | New SPCX shares (M) | Tesla holders' stake | Headline premium | Gain to Tesla holders, no synergies | Synergies SpaceX holders need to break even | Musk voting power* | Musk economic stake | Change in Musk's wealth, no synergies |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 2.588 | 10,229 | 43.0% | $0B | $0B | $0B | 76.4% | 33.1% | $0B |
| 10% | 2.847 | 11,252 | 45.3% | $148B | $81B | $148B | 75.5% | 32.5% | -$22B |
| 20% | 3.105 | 12,275 | 47.5% | $296B | $156B | $296B | 74.7% | 31.9% | -$42B |
| 30% | 3.364 | 13,297 | 49.5% | $444B | $224B | $444B | 74.0% | 31.3% | -$61B |
| 35% | 3.494 | 13,809 | 50.4% | $518B | $257B | $518B | 73.6% | 31.1% | -$69B |

*\*Assumes SpaceX acquires Tesla, keeps its dual-class charter, and issues Class A shares (1 vote) to Tesla holders. Musk's SpaceX position is taken from the prospectus (849.5M A plus 5,219.1M B, excluding 350M B options) and applied to the current share count (15-Oct-2026 cover pages). On that basis he has ~86% of SpaceX's votes as of October 2026. The prospectus gave 82.4% at the IPO, and the press says "about 85%". Tesla: 699.6M shares (DEF 14A); the 423.7M unearned award shares are excluded.*

Three things stand out:

1. **The 20-35% premium range is very large in dollars.** About half of the headline premium is funded by Tesla holders themselves, because the SpaceX shares they receive are diluted. That is why the "gain" column is roughly half the "headline" column.
2. **Contribution brackets the fight** (Figure 1). Tesla is 43% of market value, 50% of TTM adjusted EBITDA and 31% of run-rate adjusted EBITDA. On TTM EBITDA, Tesla holders deserve ~50%, which is a ~33% premium. On Q3 run-rate, they would get less than the market already gives them. Expect Tesla's committee to argue TTM and SpaceX's to argue run-rate.
3. **Control and economics pull Musk in opposite directions.** In a SpaceX-led deal he controls the combined company at any plausible premium, but he personally pays for the premium (Figure 2). A low premium serves his wealth, and that is exactly why Tesla's committee has to be seen to bargain hard.

**Not reconciled:** Reuters quoted an "illustrative 0.7 of one SpaceX share" ratio attributed to Barclays, with Musk at 57-58% of the vote. That ratio cannot be squared with share prices of $144.75 and $374.58, and those voting figures do not match a dual-class SpaceX survivor. They may assume a different structure (for example a one-share-one-vote holding company, where Musk would hold only ~31-33%). I have not used them.

## 5. Structure and consideration

- **SpaceX buys Tesla (reported option).** Tesla becomes a subsidiary and SpaceX's dual-class charter survives. Musk's control is kept, and SpaceX's own approvals are a formality, because Musk controls its vote.
- **A new holding company (reported option).** The holdco's voting structure becomes a term in its own right: a dual-class holdco keeps Musk in control, while a single-class holdco would cost him control. Tesla's new credit agreements pre-approve Tesla becoming a wholly owned subsidiary of a holding company controlled by "Permitted Holders" (Musk and named trusts).
- **Consideration:** all-stock is the reported base case. SpaceX has ~$90B of cash and securities, but against $19.6B of capex a quarter, cash consideration is unlikely to be a large part.
- **Tesla's debt:** the 29-Sep facilities ($20B delayed-draw term loan, $8B five-year revolver, $2B 364-day revolver, all undrawn) treat a Musk-controlled combination as a permitted transaction only if Tesla keeps investment-grade ratings from two of Moody's, S&P and Fitch. The test runs from the first public announcement of an agreement through day 90, and is extended while any rating is on watch. If it fails, the deal becomes a change in control. Tesla's reported ratings are Baa3, BBB and BBB (not verified with the agencies), so Moody's is one notch from the line.

## 6. Governance and minority shareholders

- **Musk is on both sides.** He is CEO of both companies, controls SpaceX and owns 19.8% of Tesla (beneficial basis). Both companies have formed special committees. Membership and advisors are not public, and Ehrenpreis's seat on both boards will need to be addressed.
- **Process taint.** Reuters reports that insiders called Musk's public endorsement a "mess" that could invite scrutiny and add weeks. The SolarCity case is the reminder. Tesla's 2016 purchase of the company was upheld as fair after years of litigation, but the court faulted the lack of a special committee.
- **The 2025 pay award is a conflict to price.** The proxy says Earned Shares vest on a change in control, while unearned shares are forfeited. Separately, 286.4M restricted shares from the 2018 award vest early on a change in control while Musk is serving. At $374.58 those shares are worth ~$107B. Bloomberg puts the potential gain at more than $110B. Whether a SpaceX combination counts as a change in control depends on how it is structured, which gives Musk a personal interest in the structure itself.
- **Texas, not Delaware.** Both companies are Texas corporations: Tesla moved in 2024, and SpaceX reincorporated on 14-Feb-2024. Texas's 2025 reforms (SB 29) codified the business judgment rule. They also let a company ask the business court to rule *in advance* on whether a committee is independent, a route that no court has yet applied (per SMU's governance centre). And they allow a derivative-suit ownership threshold of up to 3%, which Tesla adopted in 2025 (reported). Delaware's approach of approval by both an independent committee and a majority of the minority (MFW, now codified by SB 21) is not required in Texas. **Whether Tesla's committee insists on a majority-of-the-minority vote anyway is the single best signal of process quality.** On 19-Dec-2025 the Delaware Supreme Court reinstated Musk's 2018 award in *Tornetta* (awarding the plaintiff $1 in nominal damages). That removes one overhang but sets no Texas precedent.
- **The Tesla vote.** Musk says approval needs a majority of all outstanding shares, so shares that are not voted count as votes against. I ran the arithmetic with Musk's 699.6M shares voting for and the 423.7M unearned shares voted in proportion to all other votes cast (my reading of the proxy mechanics). If 70% of the other 2,829M shares vote, ~51% of them must vote for; at 60% turnout, ~58%. Turnout is the real risk. A majority-of-the-minority condition would be much harder.
- **TBOC §21.606** (affiliated-shareholder business combinations) needs two-thirds of disinterested shares unless an exemption applies. It is triggered at 20% ownership. Musk's 19.8% beneficial stake sits just below that, though whether he is "affiliated" is a legal question I cannot answer.

## 8. Market reaction

From the 20-Oct close (just before the call) to the 22-Oct close, SPCX fell 2.1% ($147.79 → $144.75) and TSLA rose 0.2% ($373.71 → $374.58). The ratio widened from 2.529x to 2.588x: Tesla outperformed by 2.3%. The path was volatile. On 21-Oct SPCX rose 7.8% and TSLA 3.4%, after SpaceX's results and Musk's comments. On 22-Oct SPCX fell 9.1% and TSLA 3.1%, on a day when large-cap tech also fell (NVDA -4.4%, AMD -8.1%). Read literally, a 2.3% relative move is consistent with the market pricing a low chance of a sizeable premium. But two days of noisy prices and SpaceX's own earnings make that unreliable, and I would not build a probability on it.

## 9. Risks

- **No deal.** Musk himself warns against assuming certainty. The committees may decline, or settle for contracts such as a Terafab cost-sharing agreement instead.
- **Premium too low, vote fails.** Because unvoted shares count against, broker non-votes and index-fund policies matter more than headline support.
- **Premium too high, SpaceX holders are diluted** for synergies that are mostly available by contract already.
- **Litigation:** in Texas the rules are untested, and the 3% threshold narrows derivative suits but not direct class claims.
- **Political and foreign-regulatory risk:** China leverage over Tesla Shanghai, midterm-driven oversight (3-Nov), and Starlink's role in Ukraine.
- **Credit:** a ratings slip during the test window turns $30B of Tesla standby liquidity into a default trigger.
- **Valuation:** both stocks trade at 95-130x adjusted EBITDA, so the exchange ratio can move 10% in a week, as it nearly did in the week of 20 Oct.

## 10. What to watch

- **An 8-K (Item 1.01 or 8.01) or Rule 425 filing:** the first terms, structure and ratio.
- **Whether Tesla adopts a majority-of-the-minority condition**, or files a Texas §21.4161 petition on its committee's independence.
- **The names of the committee members and advisors**, and whether Ehrenpreis recuses himself.
- **Rating actions on Tesla** (Moody's Baa3 is the swing vote).
- **The Tesla annual meeting (3-Dec)** and the December expiry of the lock-up on Tesla's SpaceX shares.
- **Positioning:** none until there are terms. Once a fixed ratio *R* is announced, the spread is *R* × SPCX - TSLA. Whether it is worth trading depends on the vote math above, not on antitrust.

## What could not be verified

- Committee members, advisors, formation dates and any term sheet: none are public.
- Reuters' and Bloomberg's sourced reporting (SpaceX made the approach; the structures under consideration; the H1-2027 target) is unconfirmed by the companies.
- The Barclays "0.7" illustrative ratio and the 57-58% Musk vote estimates could not be reconciled with share prices or structures (see §4).
- Musk's current SpaceX holdings: I used prospectus figures on the current share count (15-Oct-2026 cover pages), so his ~86% voting estimate may be slightly off.
- Tesla's and SpaceX's credit ratings are from secondary reports.
- The Tesla charter's vote requirement and the 2025 adoption of the 3% derivative threshold rely on Musk's statement and secondary summaries; I did not read the charter itself.
- Whether TBOC §21.606, Rule 13e-3 or the pay-award change-in-control clauses apply depends on the final structure. Those are legal questions, and I have flagged them without answering them.
- The synergy multiple (20x after tax) is an assumption used only for scale.
